Section 1

The Thesis

Stateless AI systems give contradictory guidance to the same person over time, and the failure gets worse — not better — the longer they use it. Better models don't fix this. Only explicit epistemic governance does.

Everything in the research supports one of three sub-claims below. If a piece of evidence doesn't connect to one of these, it's not part of the paper. If a sub-claim has no surviving evidence after validation, it gets cut.

Why this paper needs to exist: Everyone assumes the memory problem is solved by giving models memory. Your data shows that unmanaged memory — context without governance — compounds drift rather than resolving it. Continuity is not coherence. Retention is not integrity.

Section 3

Evidence Scoring Rubric

Score every claim 0–3. Only claims scoring 2+ go into a paper. Claims scoring 1 can appear in supplementary material with appropriate hedging. Claims scoring 0 get cut entirely.

Score Label Criteria What You Need
3 Reproducible Harness exists, you can rerun it, numbers match the claim within tolerance Working script + matching output + no manual cherry-picking
2 Artifact-Backed Raw results exist on disk, traceable to specific code, but may not be recently re-verified Result files + code that produced them + clear provenance
1 Directional Evidence supports the claim's direction but numbers are soft, scope is uncertain, or the methodology has known gaps Partial data + acknowledged limitations + no overclaiming
0 Narrative Only The claim exists in docs but has no artifact, no rerun path, and no traceable origin Cut it. Do not publish.
Rule 1
Numbers Need Artifacts

Any specific number in a doc (0.34 mean consistency, 59.5% flip rate, 12.4% hard rate at 3+ months) must trace to a script output, database query, or result file. If it traces only to another doc page that also has no source, it's a 0.

Rule 2
Prose Doesn't Stack

A claim appearing in three different docs doesn't make it more true. It makes it more dangerous. One source of truth per number. Everything else is a citation, not independent evidence.

Rule 3
Hedging Is Mandatory Below 2

If a claim scores 1, it can still appear — but it must say "directional finding" or "preliminary" or "methodology limitation noted." No unqualified strong language for score-1 claims.

Rule 4
Separate What You Measured From What You Infer

The contradiction rate is measured. "This means users are harmed" is inference. Both can appear in the paper, but the boundary must be explicit. Never let an inference inherit the score of a measurement.

Open Gaps

Things the paper needs that don't exist yet. Be honest about these. A paper that acknowledges its gaps is stronger than one that hides them.

Gap 1
No Independent Dataset

All corpus data is from your own GPT usage. This is ecologically valid but not independently reproducible. Another researcher can't verify your numbers without your conversations.

Mitigation: describe methodology precisely enough that someone could replicate with their own export. Consider publishing anonymized aggregate stats.

Gap 2
No A/B: Governed vs Ungoverned

You have the ungoverned baseline (GPT corpus). You have the governed system (Aether/CRT). But you don't have a controlled comparison where the same prompts go to both and you measure the difference in contradiction rate.

Mitigation: your real-time testing over the next few days can partially address this. Log both Aether's governed responses and what a stateless API call would produce for the same input.

Gap 3
Arc-Level Detection Is Crude

Keyword-based stance detection with no NLI. The structural finding (93% span 3+ turns) is solid. The precision (what % of detected flips are real) is unknown.

Mitigation: manually verify 50–100 detected flips. Report precision. Or add NLI-based filtering.

Gap 4
No Activation-Level Evidence

You claim framing pressure causes trajectory shifts. You have behavioral evidence (different outputs). You don't have internal evidence (different activations). The heatmap harness is designed but not built.

Mitigation: build it, or explicitly scope the paper to behavioral evidence only and flag activation work as future direction.

Gap 5
CRT Hasn't Been Stress-Tested At Scale

The system works for one user over months. It hasn't been tested with multiple users, adversarial inputs, or high-volume concurrent sessions.

Mitigation: scope the paper as "proof of concept" not "production system." This is honest and sufficient for a research contribution.

Gap 6
Thinking-Model Sample Sizes

The strongest "scale doesn't fix it" datapoint (gpt-5-1-thinking at 20.1% hard rate) comes from only 179 pairs. Directionally compelling but not statistically conclusive at conventional significance levels.

Mitigation: state sample sizes. Use language like "directional evidence" not "conclusive finding."

Section 14

How to Write a Research Paragraph

Every paragraph in a research paper does one job: make a claim and back it up. If a paragraph doesn't do this, it's either filler or it belongs in a different section. Here's the formula.

The Structure
Claim → Evidence → Interpretation → Connection
  1. Claim (1 sentence): State what you're asserting. Be specific. No hedging yet.
  2. Evidence (1–3 sentences): The data, the measurement, the artifact. Numbers, methods, sources. This is where your credibility lives.
  3. Interpretation (1–2 sentences): What does the evidence mean? This is where you explain why the reader should care. Hedging goes here if needed.
  4. Connection (1 sentence): Link to the next point, the broader thesis, or the implication. Gives the reader a reason to keep reading.
Common Mistakes
What Kills Credibility
  • Claim with no evidence: "This is a significant finding." — Says who? Show the number.
  • Evidence with no interpretation: "The value was 0.34." — So what? What does that mean for the reader?
  • Hedging the claim instead of the interpretation: "It might possibly be the case that..." — State the claim cleanly, then hedge the interpretation.
  • Stacking adjectives instead of evidence: "The groundbreaking, novel, unprecedented approach..." — If it's novel, the evidence will show that. You don't need to announce it.
  • Citing yourself as proof: Saying the same thing in three docs doesn't make it more true. One source, one number, one artifact.

Side-by-Side: Weak vs Strong

Weak
Vague, No Evidence, Overclaims

Stateless AI systems have a significant problem with contradictions. Our research shows that this is a major issue that affects many users. The system frequently gives different advice across sessions, which can be very harmful. This demonstrates the clear need for a governance architecture like CRT.

What's wrong: No numbers. "Significant," "major," "frequently," "very" are unsupported adjectives. "Our research shows" without citing what it showed. "Clear need" is the writer's opinion presented as conclusion. The reader has no reason to believe any of this.

Strong
Specific, Grounded, Measured

[Claim] Cross-session contradiction rates in stateless AI systems increase with usage duration. [Evidence] Analysis of 8,544 response pairs across 1,275 sessions found a hard contradiction rate of 7% for pairs within one month, rising to 12.4% for pairs separated by three or more months (Table 5, v1 corpus). A stricter v2 reanalysis with query-intent filtering confirmed 47 genuine contradictions among 453 surviving pairs (10.4%), concentrated in decision-heavy domains. [Interpretation] This temporal amplification suggests the failure is not random but structural: the longer a user relies on the system, the more likely they are to receive contradictory guidance on the same topic. [Connection] The time-dependent nature of this failure motivates the continuity auditing component of the CRT architecture (Section 6).

What's right: Specific claim (contradiction rates increase with time). Exact numbers with sources (8,544 pairs, 7% vs 12.4%, Table 5). Interpretation hedged appropriately ("suggests"). Connection to the next section. The reader can verify every number.

Templates You Can Use

Empirical Finding
When You Have Numbers
[Topic] exhibits [specific pattern]. In our analysis of [N items] across [scope], [metric] was [value] (Table/Figure X). This [exceeds/falls below] [baseline or expectation] by [amount], indicating [interpretation]. [Connection to thesis or next finding].
Methodology
When Explaining How
To measure [what], we [method]. [N items] were [processed/filtered/compared] using [tool/technique], producing [output type]. [Key parameter] was set to [value] because [reason]. This approach [advantage], though [limitation/caveat].
Comparison
When Contrasting Two Things
[System A] achieves [metric A], while [System B] achieves [metric B] under [same conditions]. The [difference/gap] of [amount] is [significant because / explained by / limited by] [reason]. This comparison [supports/complicates/extends] the claim that [thesis connection].
Limitation
When Being Honest About Gaps
This finding is subject to [limitation]. [Specific constraint: sample size, single user, keyword detection, etc.]. As a result, [metric] should be interpreted as [upper bound / directional / preliminary] rather than [conclusive / generalizable]. [What would strengthen it: larger corpus, NLI validation, independent dataset, etc.].
The One Rule
Every Sentence Earns Its Place

Read each sentence and ask: "If I deleted this, would the paragraph lose information?" If the answer is no, delete it. Research writing is not about sounding smart. It's about being clear, being specific, and letting the evidence do the work. If the data is strong, you don't need adjectives. If the data is weak, adjectives won't save it.
