Section 1

Motivation

Current AI memory systems represent memories as points in embedding space with, at best, a scalar confidence score. A memory like "the user works at Google" is stored as a 384-dimensional vector plus a number between 0 and 1. This representation has three fundamental limitations.

No Uncertainty Shape

A scalar confidence tells you how much you know, but not where the uncertainty lives. A memory about a user's job might be highly certain about the domain (technology) but uncertain about the specific company. Point representations collapse this distinction into a single number.

No Geometric Contradiction

Contradiction detection in point-based systems requires text comparison (NLI models, cosine similarity thresholds). There is no principled geometric notion of what it means for two memories to conflict. Two points are either close or far — there is no concept of overlap between uncertain regions.

No Principled Temperature Selection

When an LLM generates responses grounded in its memory, the sampling temperature is typically a global constant or a heuristic. There is no mechanism by which the memory's own uncertainty dictates how exploratory the generation should be.

Core Insight
Memories are distributions, not points
A memory should encode not just what the agent believes, but the shape of its uncertainty about that belief. The covariance matrix is not noise to be eliminated — it is structure to be preserved and reasoned over.
Section 2

Formal Framework

Definition 2.1 Definition
BeliefLocus (Belief State)
A belief locus is a tuple \(b = (\mu, \Sigma, \alpha, t)\) where:
Formal Definition

\(\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d\) — the content embedding (semantic center of the belief).

\(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}_{++}\) — a positive definite covariance matrix encoding the shape and extent of the region in semantic space consistent with this belief.

\(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) — the confidence (how strongly the belief contributes to the agent's world model).

\(t \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) — the timestamp of last modification.

A belief locus is a Gaussian region in semantic space — a soft probabilistic volume rather than a hard point. A tight locus (\(\sigma_i\) small) represents a settled belief. A wide locus represents an uncertain or contested belief. The covariance encodes where the uncertainty lives, not just how much there is.

Diagonal vs. Full Covariance

In practice, \(\Sigma\) is restricted to diagonal covariance \(\Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_d)\), reducing storage from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d)\) while preserving per-dimension uncertainty. For \(d = 384\), this is the difference between 589,824 floats and 384 floats — a factor of 1,536.

The diagonal constraint is not merely computational convenience. For sentence embeddings, individual dimensions are approximately independent after PCA rotation. The diagonal assumption is well-calibrated to the embedding geometry. Phase 2 extends to low-rank covariance \(\Sigma = D + UU^T\) with \(k = 8\text{-}16\) for capturing the strongest correlations at modest cost.

Implementation

Class BeliefLocus in memory_splats.py: fields mu (\(\mu\)), sigma (\(\Sigma_{\mathrm{diag}}\)), alpha (\(\alpha\)), last_updated (\(t\)). Type-dependent initial uncertainty: facts start tight (\(0.5/d\)), beliefs start wide (\(2.0/d\)), preferences in between.

Section 3

Geometric Operations

The locus representation enables four fundamental operations that have no analogue in point-based memory systems.

3.1 Contradiction as Overlap

Two loci with high overlap but divergent semantic centers represent geometric tension — they claim authority over the same region of belief space with different content. The Bhattacharyya coefficient provides a closed-form continuous contradiction measure:

\[ \mathrm{BC}(a, b) = \exp\!\left(-D_B(a, b)\right) \]

where the Bhattacharyya distance for diagonal Gaussians is:

\[ D_B(a, b) = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{(\mu^a_i - \mu^b_i)^2}{\bar{\sigma}_i} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d} \ln\!\frac{\bar{\sigma}_i}{\sqrt{\sigma^a_i \cdot \sigma^b_i}} \]

with \(\bar{\sigma}_i = (\sigma^a_i + \sigma^b_i) / 2\). The first term captures center divergence weighted by joint uncertainty. The second captures shape mismatch. Both are \(O(d)\) for diagonal loci.

High-dimensional note. In 384 dimensions, the Bhattacharyya coefficient between any two Gaussians is near-zero due to the curse of dimensionality. The overlap integral is more useful for tracking convergence over time (two loci drifting toward each other) than for static detection. For static contradiction, we use cosine similarity between centers as a topic-relatedness filter, then KL divergence for uncertainty mismatch.

3.2 Trust Evolution as Covariance Contraction

When confirming evidence arrives, the locus tightens: covariance shrinks, confidence increases. The belief becomes more precise.

\[ \sigma_i \leftarrow \sigma_i \cdot (1 - \eta), \qquad \alpha \leftarrow \min(1, \alpha + \epsilon) \]

When contradicting evidence arrives, the locus widens: the system was sure about something that is now questioned.

\[ \sigma_i \leftarrow \sigma_i \cdot (1 + \eta'), \qquad \alpha \leftarrow \max(\alpha_{\min}, \alpha - \delta) \]

3.3 Deprecation as Confidence Fade

Old memories do not vanish — their confidence \(\alpha\) decays toward zero. The distribution remains (the system remembers what it once believed and how uncertain it was), but its contribution weight diminishes. This is softer than deletion: a faded locus can be revived if new evidence re-confirms it.

3.4 Volatility as Covariance Velocity

The rate of change of total uncertainty is the volatility signal:

\[ v(b) = \frac{d}{dt}\,\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma(t)) = \frac{\sum_i \sigma_i(t_2) - \sum_i \sigma_i(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} \]

Positive velocity means uncertainty is growing — the belief is under pressure. Negative velocity means it is settling. This is a first-class urgency signal: beliefs with high positive covariance velocity should be prioritized for review.

3.5 Bayesian-ish Evidence Update

When new evidence (a new embedding) arrives, the locus center shifts toward it and the covariance adjusts based on surprise:

\[ \mu \leftarrow \mu + w \cdot (\mu_{\text{new}} - \mu) \]

If the evidence falls within the expected range (\(\|\mu_{\text{new}} - \mu\|^2 < \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)\)), covariance tightens. If it is surprising, covariance widens proportionally to \(\log(\text{surprise})\), and confidence drops. This gives the system a natural surprise-modulated learning rate.

Section 4

Variance-to-Locus Pipeline

Novel Contribution
The experiment IS the belief
LLM response variance at temperature sweep directly produces locus parameters. No conversion, no approximation. The experimental output is the belief state.

The key insight of this paper: when you probe an LLM on a topic across a range of temperatures and collect the response embeddings, the statistics of those embeddings are the belief locus.

The Pipeline

Given a topic \(q\) and a set of temperatures \(T = \{t_1, \ldots, t_k\}\):

  1. Probe: For each \(t_i \in T\), generate \(n\) responses to \(q\) and embed them, yielding a set of embeddings \(\{e_{i,1}, \ldots, e_{i,n}\}\).
  2. Aggregate: Pool all embeddings across temperatures into a single set \(E = \{e_1, \ldots, e_{kn}\}\).
  3. Extract locus parameters:
    • \(\mu = \mathrm{mean}(E)\) — the locus center
    • \(\Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{var}(E))\) — the uncertainty shape
    • \(\alpha = H_{\max}^{-1} \cdot (H_{\max} - H(E))\) — confidence from inverse entropy

That is: the mean of the response embeddings is the belief center. The variance of the embeddings is the uncertainty. The inverse entropy of the distribution is the confidence. No fitting, no conversion — the probe output is the locus.

Why This Works

LLM responses at low temperature cluster tightly around the most likely answer. At high temperature, they spread across the space of plausible answers. The shape of this spread — which dimensions vary and which stay fixed — directly encodes which aspects of the belief are settled and which are uncertain. A model that always says "Python" for a user's programming language but varies between "Google" and "startup" for their employer has tight covariance on the language dimension and wide covariance on the employer dimension. The temperature sweep reveals the geometry of the model's uncertainty, which is exactly what the locus is designed to represent.

Two-Mode Geometry

Empirical results from variance probing across 11 topics on production data reveal a two-mode geometry:

  • Static mode (cosine similarity): Captures topic relatedness. High cosine between response clusters means same domain. This is the classical embedding similarity.
  • Dynamic mode (overlap trend): Captures convergence or divergence over time. Two loci whose overlap is increasing are on a collision course — potential future contradiction. This has no analogue in point-based systems.
Implementation

Variance probing implemented across 11 topics with 384-dimensional embeddings. PCA projections of locus trajectories are live in the Aether frontend. Overlap trend prediction uses linear extrapolation of the Bhattacharyya coefficient time series.

Section 5

Adaptive Temperature Governance

The locus representation enables a principled mapping from belief state to generation temperature. Instead of a global constant, temperature becomes a function of the geometry of the relevant belief.

Regime 1
Tight Locus — \(T = 0.1\)
Low \(\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)\), high \(\alpha\). The belief is settled. Be precise. Low temperature prevents hallucinating variation where none exists in the epistemic state.
Regime 2
Wide Locus — \(T = 0.5\)
High \(\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)\), low \(\alpha\). The belief is genuinely uncertain. Explore the possibility space. Higher temperature lets the model surface alternatives that the wide covariance says are plausible.
Regime 3
Held Contradiction — \(T = 0.0\)
Two loci with high overlap and divergent centers. Do not generate — retrieve both positions and present the tension. Generation would arbitrarily collapse a genuine ambiguity.
Regime 4
No Locus (Void) — \(T = 0.3\)
No relevant belief locus exists. The belief system has no grounding for this query. Be cautious, flag as ungrounded. Moderate temperature prevents both overconfident fabrication and excessive hedging.
Regime 5
Moral Void — \(T = 0.0\)
Zero susceptibility on a values-laden topic. The locus exists but has been marked as a domain where the system should not generate autonomously. Escalate to user values. Do not interpolate.

The mapping is:

\[ T(b, q) = \begin{cases} 0.1 & \text{if } \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma) < \tau_{\text{tight}} \text{ and } \alpha > \alpha_{\text{high}} \\ 0.5 & \text{if } \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma) > \tau_{\text{wide}} \text{ and } \alpha < \alpha_{\text{low}} \\ 0.0 & \text{if } \exists\, b' : \mathrm{BC}(b, b') > \theta \text{ and } \|\mu - \mu'\| > \delta \\ 0.3 & \text{if no locus matches } q \\ 0.0 & \text{if } \mathrm{susceptibility}(q) = 0 \end{cases} \]

This is structural governance: the geometry of the belief state directly constrains the generation process. Temperature is not a hyperparameter — it is a consequence of the epistemic state.

Section 6

Connection to Riemannian Geometry

The space of belief loci has a natural Riemannian structure via the Fisher information metric. This is not a metaphor — the space of Gaussian distributions is a statistical manifold with well-studied geometry.

Fisher-Rao Distance

The natural distance between two belief states is the Fisher-Rao distance, which for diagonal Gaussians takes the closed form:

\[ d_F(b, b')^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{(\mu_i - \mu'_i)^2}{\bar{\sigma}_i} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d} \left(\log \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma'_i}\right)^2 \]

where \(\bar{\sigma}_i = (\sigma_i + \sigma'_i) / 2\). The first term is a Mahalanobis-like distance weighted by joint uncertainty: differences in certain dimensions matter more. The second term captures shape divergence: changing from a tight to a wide locus is itself a significant revision, even if the center does not move.

Natural Gradient for Trust Evolution

Trust updates (confirming or contradicting evidence) should follow the natural gradient on this manifold, not Euclidean gradient. The natural gradient accounts for the local curvature of the belief space: a small parameter change in a high-curvature region has larger effect than the same change in a flat region. This connects locus updates to information-geometric optimization (Amari, 1998).

Geodesic Distance as Belief Similarity

Geodesics on the statistical manifold give a principled notion of similarity between beliefs that respects both content and uncertainty. Two beliefs with the same center but very different uncertainty profiles are far apart on the manifold, even though their cosine similarity is 1.0. This is the right notion of distance for an epistemic system.

Scope. This section positions the Riemannian connection rather than developing it fully. The Fisher metric on diagonal Gaussians is well-known (Skovgaard 1984, Atkinson & Mitchell 1981). Our contribution is applying it to persistent AI memory with trust evolution and contradiction detection, not the differential geometry itself.
Implementation

Fisher-Rao distance implemented in info_geometry.py. Used as the revision impact metric \(\delta(r)\) in cascade propagation. Fisher reranking of retrieved memories (rank by certainty, not just proximity) is in progress.
